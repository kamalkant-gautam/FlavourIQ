The Beverage Guide

From a glass of water to a vintage whisky: what a beverage is, how it is classified, and how to speak about it like a professional. Built for beginners and experienced hospitality hands alike.

What is a beverage?

A beverage is any liquid prepared for human consumption by drinking. It quenches thirst, delivers nutrients or energy, carries flavour and is central to social ritual, from morning chai to a celebratory toast. In a hotel, beverages are one half of F&B (Food & Beverage), with their own departments, cost controls, licences and service standards.

Why we drink

Hydration, nourishment, energy, comfort, social bonding, celebration, medicine and religion or tradition.

The one big split

Everything divides on a single question: does it contain ethanol (alcohol)? If not, it is non-alcoholic; if yes, it is alcoholic.

Drink vs beverage

In everyday speech they are the same. In the trade, “drinks” often implies alcohol (“drinks menu”) while “beverage” is the formal umbrella for everything.

Brief history

Water and milk came first; fermented drinks (mead, beer, wine) appear about 7,000-9,000 years ago; tea in China, coffee in Ethiopia and Yemen, distillation in the medieval period, and carbonation in the 1700s.

Non-alcoholic beverages

Drinks with no alcohol (or under 0.5% ABV in most regulations), grouped by the job they do for the guest: stimulate, refresh, nourish, or offer something modern and zero-proof. Tap any card for detail and examples.

Alcoholic beverages

Alcohol (ethanol) is made by fermentation: yeast turns sugar into alcohol and CO₂. Fermented drinks top out near 15% ABV, because yeast dies beyond that. To go higher, the liquid is distilled, heated so alcohol vapour is collected and condensed. Strength is shown as ABV (alcohol by volume).

Unique fact: is soup a food or a beverage?

Short answer: soup is classified as food. It is cooked in the kitchen, sits on the food menu as a course, usually holds solids (vegetables, meat, noodles), is eaten with a spoon and is judged by how filling it is. A beverage is defined by being drunk, mainly to hydrate or refresh.

The grey zone: a clear consommé sipped from a cup, a chilled gazpacho in a glass, or bone broth served as a hot drink can feel like a beverage. Trade practice still files them under food because the kitchen makes and costs them. My view: use a three-part test: how is it consumed, who prepares it, and does it contain solids? Spoon + kitchen + solids = food. Sip + bar/beverage station + liquid = beverage.

Know it, serve it

Useful on your first day in the industry and on your thousandth service. Newcomers get the logic; professionals get the checkpoints.

If you are new

Start with the hierarchy above. Ask three questions about any drink: Is there alcohol? Is it hot or cold? How is it made (brewed, pressed, fermented, distilled, blended)? Those answers place almost any drink and predict its taste.

If you work the floor

Right glass, right temperature, right garnish. Standard pours (30/60 ml), mise-en-place, allergen awareness (dairy, nuts, sulphites, gluten in beer), responsible service of alcohol, and always offering a zero-proof option.

If you run the numbers

Beverage pour cost usually sits around 18-25%. Track wastage, breakage, par stock and portion control; mocktails and house-made sodas carry some of the best margins on a menu.

Pairing principles

Match weight with weight, mirror or contrast flavours, acid cuts fat, sweet tames spice, tannin loves protein. Sparkling and tea cleanse the palate between courses.

Types of Beverages Explained

▶A Brief History of AlcoholTED-Ed
Core Classification:
  • Fermented (3%-15% ABV): Beer (grains, hops, water, yeast) and Wine (fermented grape must).
  • Distilled Spirits (35%-55%+ ABV): Ethanol concentrated via heat separation. Includes Whiskey (aged grain mash), Vodka (neutral spirit), Gin (botanical/juniper redistillation), Rum (sugarcane/molasses), and Tequila/Mezcal (agave).
  • Aromatized & Fortified: Vermouths, amaros, and cordials enhanced with botanical infusions and added sugars.

How Beer, Wine and Whisky Are Made

▶How Scotch Whisky is Made: Grain to GlassVisitScotland▶How Whiskey is Made (Made in a Day)National Geographic
Production Flow:
  • Wine: Harvest → Crushing/Pressing → Primary Fermentation → Clarification → Aging → Bottling.
  • Beer: Malting → Mashing (starch conversion) → Boil with hops → Yeast fermentation → Carbonation.
  • Whisky: Grain mashing → Fermentation (~8% ABV wash) → Pot/Column Still Distillation (60%-70%+ ABV) → Oak cask maturation → Dilution & bottling.

Bartending Basics: Shake, Stir, Build

▶How To Mix Every Cocktail (Method Mastery)Epicurious
Execution Rules:
  • Build: Assemble directly in glass over ice (e.g., Highball, Spritz). One smooth vertical lift with the bar spoon to blend without reducing carbonation.
  • Stir: For clear, spirit-forward drinks (Martini, Manhattan, Negroni). Keep the spoon back flush to the glass; rotate smoothly for 25-30 seconds for clear, un-aerated dilution.
  • Shake: For formulas with citrus, syrup, cream, or egg white (Sours, Margaritas). Hard horizontal/elliptical shake for 10-12 seconds; double strain to catch micro-ice shards.

Mocktail Principles and Formulations

Standard Balancing Ratios:
  • Zero-Proof Sour Ratio (2 : 1 : 1): 2 oz Base (chilled tea, cold brew, or non-alcoholic spirit) + 1 oz Fresh Citrus (lemon/lime) + 1 oz Sweet/Syrup balance. Shaken and fine-strained.
  • Highball / Spritz Balance: 1.5 oz Flavored fruit purée or shrub + 0.75 oz Fresh acid + 4 oz Effervescent soda/tonic. Built over fresh cubes.
