THE BASICS

One plant, almost every tea you've had

Green, yellow, white, oolong, black and dark (including pu-erh) all come from the same evergreen shrub, Camellia sinensis. Nothing about the plant changes between them — what changes is how the leaf is handled the moment it's picked: how much it withers, how it's shaped, and above all, how much it's allowed to oxidise before the process is stopped. That single variable — oxidation — is the entire reason tea comes in so many colours and flavours.

FROM BUSH TO CUP

How tea is actually made

Six broad steps turn a fresh leaf into every style on this page — how far it goes through them, and in what order, decides which category it lands in.

1. Pluck

Two leaves and a bud, hand-picked at the shoot tip.

2. Wither

Leaves lose moisture, going soft and pliable.

3. Roll / Shape

Bruises the leaf, releasing juices and enzymes.

4. Oxidise

Open air turns leaf enzymes darker and stronger — stopped early for green, longer for black.

5. Fix / Dry

Heat locks in the flavour and halts oxidation for good.

6. Sort & Grade

Leaves are graded by size and quality before packing.

White Tea

Minimally processed

Origin: Fujian, China — Fuding & Zhenghe counties

Oxidation

Tap to see history, brewing & benefits →

History

Prized at the Song dynasty imperial court a thousand years ago; the modern Silver Needle style was refined for export trade in the 1800s.

Notable styles: Silver Needle (Bai Hao Yinzhen), White Peony (Bai Mudan)

Brewing

75–85°C · 2g per 200ml · steep 4–5 min

Benefits

  • Highest antioxidant retention of any tea due to minimal processing
  • Gentle, low caffeine
Know this: made only from young buds and first leaves, still coated in fine silvery down.

Yellow Tea

Lightly, slowly oxidised

Origin: Hunan & Sichuan, China — Junshan Island, Mengding Mountain

Oxidation

Tap to see history, brewing & benefits →

History

A rare imperial tribute tea through the Ming and Qing dynasties. Its extra "sealing yellow" step is slow and labour-intensive, which kept it niche even in China.

Notable styles: Junshan Yinzhen, Huoshan Huangya

Brewing

75–85°C · 2g per 200ml · steep 2–3 min

Benefits

  • Similar antioxidants to green tea, milder on the stomach
  • Smoother, less grassy than green
Know this: leaves rest wrapped in cloth or paper after firing, mellowing the flavour without full oxidation — one of the rarest tea styles left.

Green Tea

Unoxidised

Origin: China (Zhejiang, Fujian) & Japan (Uji, Shizuoka)

Oxidation

Tap to see history, brewing & benefits →

History

Legend credits Emperor Shennong in 2737 BCE. Buddhist monks carried it to Japan by the 9th century, where it evolved into matcha and the tea ceremony.

Notable styles: Dragon Well (Longjing), Sencha, Matcha, Gunpowder

Brewing

70–80°C · 2g per 200ml · steep 2–3 min

Benefits

  • Rich in EGCG catechins, a potent antioxidant
  • L-theanine gives calm, focused energy
Know this: never use boiling water — it scorches the leaf and turns the cup bitter and grassy.

Oolong Tea

Semi-oxidised (10–80%)

Origin: Fujian & Guangdong, China; Taiwan's high mountains

Oxidation

Tap to see history, brewing & benefits →

History

Named "black dragon," it emerged in the Wuyi Mountains under the Qing dynasty. Taiwanese growers spent the 19th–20th centuries refining high-mountain versions.

Notable styles: Tie Guan Yin, Da Hong Pao, Dong Ding, Taiwanese High Mountain

Brewing

85–95°C · 3g per 150ml · short 3–5 min infusions, re-steeped

Benefits

  • Metabolism-supporting compounds
  • A calmer lift than coffee or black tea
Know this: oxidation ranges from 10–80%, so flavour swings from light and floral to dark and roasted.

Black Tea

Fully oxidised

Origin: China (Wuyi), India (Assam, Darjeeling), Sri Lanka, Kenya

Oxidation

Tap to see history, brewing & benefits →

History

Developed in Fujian's Wuyi Mountains in the 1600s. The British East India Company's taste for it drove colonial-era plantations across Assam from the 1830s.

Notable styles: Assam, Darjeeling, Ceylon, Keemun, Lapsang Souchong

Brewing

95–100°C · 2.5g per 200ml · steep 3–5 min

Benefits

  • Highest caffeine of the true teas
  • Theaflavins linked to heart health
Know this: the base of most blends — English Breakfast, Earl Grey and classic chai all start here.

Dark Tea (Pu-erh)

Post-fermented & aged

Origin: Yunnan, China

ProcessFermented & aged, months to decades

Tap to see history, brewing & benefits →

History

Compressed into cakes for the Ancient Tea Horse Road since the Tang dynasty, so it could survive long journeys by mule — ageing was later prized much like wine.

Notable styles: Sheng (raw, ages naturally), Shou (ripe, fast-fermented), Liu Bao

Brewing

95–100°C · rinse leaves first, then 3–5 min, re-steeps many times

Benefits

  • Traditionally used to aid digestion
  • May support healthy cholesterol levels
Know this: two styles — raw sheng, which ages naturally over years, and ripe shou, fermented quickly by humid piling.

RITUAL & SERVICE

Tea around the world

The same leaf, shaped into six very different rituals — worth knowing for anyone serving guests from these traditions.

China — Gongfu Cha

Small clay Yixing pots and many short, deliberate infusions, treating tea appreciation as a slow, attentive ritual rather than a quick drink.

Japan — Chanoyu

Matcha whisked to a froth in a ceremony rooted in wabi-sabi — imperfection, stillness, and quiet hospitality practised in dedicated tea houses.

Britain — Afternoon Tea

Black tea with milk, plus scones and finger sandwiches — a tradition credited to the Duchess of Bedford in the 1840s to bridge the gap to a late dinner.

India — Masala Chai

Black tea boiled with milk, sugar, and warming spices — cardamom, ginger, cloves, cinnamon — sold fresh by chai-wallahs on nearly every street corner.

Morocco — Atay (Mint Tea)

Green tea with fresh mint and sugar, poured from height into small glasses to build a foam — a ritual gesture of hospitality offered to every guest.

Russia — Samovar Tea

A strong concentrate (zavarka) brewed separately and diluted to taste with hot water from a samovar, often sipped with jam or a sugar cube.

WHAT'S ACTUALLY IN THE CUP

Tea's key compounds

Most of what tea is known for traces back to a handful of naturally occurring compounds — present in different amounts depending on how the leaf was processed.

Polyphenols & catechins

Plant antioxidants, most concentrated in unoxidised teas. EGCG, the standout catechin in green tea, is one of the most studied antioxidants in the plant kingdom.

Theaflavins & thearubigins

Formed as catechins oxidise during black tea processing — they give black tea its colour and are linked to cardiovascular benefits.

L-theanine

An amino acid found almost exclusively in tea. It promotes calm, focused alertness and takes the edge off caffeine's jitteriness.

Caffeine

Naturally occurring stimulant; the amount varies by tea type, leaf grade, water temperature, and steep time — not just by category.

KEEP IT FRESH

Storage & serving notes

Air, light, heat, moisture

The four enemies of tea. Store leaves airtight, away from sunlight and away from strong-smelling foods or spices, which tea absorbs readily.

Use it within its window

Green and white teas taste best within 6–12 months of production; black and oolong hold up longer. Pu-erh is the exception — it's meant to age for years.

Water quality matters

Heavily chlorinated or very hard water flattens flavour — filtered water gives a noticeably cleaner cup.

Bitterness is usually a brewing fault

Over-steeping or over-heating the water is the most common cause of a bitter cup, not the tea itself.

SPEAK THE LANGUAGE

Glossary of tea terms

Tap a term to reveal its definition.

Oxidation
The chemical reaction between leaf enzymes and oxygen that darkens the leaf and deepens flavour — the main axis separating tea types.
Flush
A harvest period. Darjeeling's "first flush" (spring) is light and floral; the "second flush" (summer) is fuller and muscatel.
Terroir
How soil, altitude and climate at a specific origin shape a tea's character — the same idea used for wine.
Orthodox vs. CTC
Orthodox tea is rolled whole-leaf by hand or gentle machine; CTC (Crush-Tear-Curl) is cut into small uniform pellets for strong, fast-brewing tea, common in chai.
Single-origin
Tea from one estate or garden, kept unblended, so its specific terroir and character come through.
Tisane
The correct term for any herbal infusion that contains no Camellia sinensis — technically not "tea" at all.
Liquor
The tea industry's term for the brewed liquid itself — its colour, clarity and taste.
Astringency
The dry, puckering sensation from tannins, especially in over-steeped or high-temperature brewed tea.
Umami
A savoury depth found especially in shade-grown Japanese greens like gyokuro and matcha.
Flowering tea
Hand-tied bundles of tea leaves and flowers that unfurl into a bloom shape when steeped in hot water.
