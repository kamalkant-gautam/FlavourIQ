Water, Explained

The most-served drink on earth hides more types, rules and secrets than you think. Still or sparkling, spring or purified, alkaline or plain: find out what is really in the glass.

Water at a glance

Plain water is just two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen (H₂O), yet it is the most-served drink in every restaurant, hotel and home. Everything you taste in a water comes from what is dissolved in it.

On EarthAbout 71% of the surface is water

But only about 2.5% of it is fresh, and less than 1% is easy to reach. That is why source protection matters.

In the bodyAbout 50-60% of an adult

Water carries nutrients, cools the body through sweat and keeps joints and organs working.

On the tongueMinerals make the taste

Pure H₂O is flat. Calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate and sodium give each water its body, softness or edge.

On the labelThe name is a rule

“Mineral”, “spring”, “purified” and “sparkling” are legal categories in many countries, so each word promises something specific.

Where bottled water comes from

Every natural water starts as rain. It sinks through soil, sand and rock for years, filtered on the way and picking up minerals, until it reaches an aquifer. People collect it at a spring or by drilling a well.

Fizz Lab: still vs sparkling

Carbonation is carbon dioxide dissolved under pressure. When the bottle opens the pressure drops and the gas escapes as bubbles. Move the slider to change the fizz, or open the bottle.

The tingle on your tongue is carbonic acid, so sparkling water is slightly acidic (about pH 4-5). Colder water holds more gas, which is why fizz fades fast as a glass warms.

pH Lab: acidic, neutral, alkaline

pH measures acidity on a scale from 0 to 14. Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is alkaline. Slide to see the indicator colour change. Real water is clear; the colour is an illustration like a pH test strip.

Most bottled waters sit between pH 6.5 and 8. “Alkaline” brands usually target 8 to 9.5.

What are minerals in water?

Minerals are natural salts that dissolve out of rock as water passes through it. They separate into tiny charged particles called ions. Watch them leave the rock and enter the glass: each one changes how the water tastes.

Calcium (Ca)

Gives a firm, slightly chalky body; the same mineral your bones are made of.

Magnesium (Mg)

A touch of bitterness and a dry finish.

Sodium (Na) and potassium (K)

Salty and mild; more sodium means a saltier taste.

Bicarbonate (HCO₃)

Softens taste and buffers acidity; it is what makes many mineral waters feel smooth.

Minerals: TDS and hardness

TDS (total dissolved solids) is the weight of minerals dissolved in a litre of water, in mg/L. It explains most of a water’s taste. These are approximate label values; they vary by source and batch, so always read the bottle. Bars are scaled for readability.

TDS bands

Under 50 very low · 50-500 low to moderate · 500-1,500 mineral-rich · over 1,500 highly mineralised.

Hardness (mg/L as CaCO₃)

0-60 soft · 61-120 moderately hard · 121-180 hard · over 180 very hard. Hard water tastes “thicker” and leaves scale.

Water service in hospitality

Water is the first thing a table receives, so it sets the tone. The best venues treat it like wine.

1. Offer

Ask: still, sparkling or tap? Present a short water list if the venue has one.

2. Present

Show the label, open the bottle at the table and pour a first small amount for the host to taste.

3. Pour

Pour from the right, two-thirds full, without touching the rim. Keep the bottle label facing the table.

4. Top up

Refill when a glass is a third full. Never let the table run dry, and offer a second bottle before the first is empty.
