A field guide

Pastry

Flour, fat, and a handful of techniques — rubbed, folded, whipped, or melted — are all that separate a hundred different pastries from each other. This guide walks through the grain, the ingredients, and the families built from them.

First things first

What is pastry, exactly?

Pastry is both a dough and everything baked from it. Unlike bread, which gets its rise and structure mostly from yeast fermentation, pastry's character comes from how fat is worked into flour — rubbed in, folded in sheets, whipped in, or melted in. That one decision is what separates a shortcrust from a croissant.

Flour

Supplies the structure. How much gluten it develops decides whether the result is tender or chewy.

Fat

Coats the flour's proteins, keeping strands short and layers separate. It's what makes pastry flaky or crumbly rather than chewy.

Technique

Rubbing, folding, whipping, or melting fat into flour — the method used decides which pastry family you end up in.

Where flour comes from

Anatomy of a wheat kernel

Every flour starts as a single wheat kernel, and every kernel has three parts. Which of the three end up in the bag decides what kind of flour you're holding.

1

Bran

The tough outer coat, rich in fibre. Left in for whole-wheat and atta flours; sifted out for refined white flours like maida.

2

Endosperm

The bulk of the kernel — mostly starch, plus the proteins that form gluten. This is what becomes refined flour on its own.

3

Germ

The embryo of the seed, high in oil and nutrients. It's removed from refined flours mainly because its fat content shortens shelf life.

Choosing the grind

Flour in pastry

The only number that matters when picking a flour for pastry is its protein content — because protein is what becomes gluten once it meets water and gets worked. More gluten means more chew and more strength; less means more tenderness and shorter crumb.

FlourProteinTypically used for
Cake / pastry flour7–9%Sweet shortcrust, choux, the most tender crumbs
Maida (refined wheat)8–10%Laminated pastry and mathri across South Asia
All-purpose flour10–12%The default for most shortcrust and general baking
Whole wheat / atta11–13%Denser, traditional doughs; includes bran and germ
Bread / strong flour12–14%Doughs that need to stretch thin without tearing, like strudel
Gluten-free blendsn/aRice, tapioca and starch mixes replicating pastry textures without wheat

As a rule of thumb: the flakier or more laminated the pastry, the more it can tolerate — or needs — a stronger flour to hold its structure through repeated folding. The shorter and crumblier the pastry, the lower the protein should be.

In smaller doses

What sugar actually does

Pastry doesn't need sugar to work — plenty of savoury doughs have none — but where it appears, it's doing more than sweetening.

Tenderises

Sugar competes with flour for water, which slows gluten formation — one more way of keeping a dough short rather than chewy.

Browns the crust

Two separate reactions give pastry its colour: caramelisation, where sugar alone breaks down under heat, and the Maillard reaction, where sugar and protein brown together at lower temperatures — responsible for most golden crusts.

Holds moisture

Sugar is hygroscopic — it draws in and holds onto water, which helps a baked pastry stay softer for longer.

Adds flavour

Beyond sweetness, sugar rounds out and carries other flavours — butter, spice, fruit — which is why even a small amount changes how a pastry tastes overall.

Getting some lift

Raising agents

Pastry rises in one of three ways: a living organism producing gas, a chemical reaction producing gas, or trapped air and steam expanding under heat.

Biological

Yeast

A living fungus that ferments sugars in the dough, releasing carbon dioxide slowly over time.

Croissant & Danish dough

Chemical

Baking powder & soda

Reacts instantly with heat or moisture to release carbon dioxide — fast, with no fermentation time needed.

Some shortcrust & quick doughs

Physical

Steam (lamination)

Folded layers of butter melt in the oven and release steam, which forces the surrounding dough sheets apart.

Puff pastry & filo

Physical

Whipped egg foam

Air beaten into eggs expands under oven heat, with no added agent at all.

Choux pastry

Layer by layer

Anatomy of a laminated pastry

Slice through any laminated pastry and the folding process is written right into the crumb — dozens of thin, alternating layers, browner at the top and paler toward the base.

1

Crust

The glossy top surface, deeply browned by direct oven heat once all its surface steam has escaped.

2

Puffed rim

The topmost layers, which puffed and separated the most — they're closest to the heat and rise first.

3

Upper flaky layers

Distinct baked sheets, each one a "leaf" created by a single fold of butter into dough during lamination.

4

Fold line

A visible seam where two rolled sections meet — a record of one of the dough's folding turns.

5

Interior layers

Softer, paler layers near the centre, shielded from direct heat and holding more moisture than the crust.

6

Lower flaky layers

Thinner, denser layers near the base, compressed under the weight of the pastry above them.

7

Base crust

The bottom surface — paler than the top, since it sits on the tray rather than facing the oven's heat directly.

The family tree

Types of pastry

Every one of these comes back to the same two ingredients, combined differently. Tap a card for how it's made and what to look for.

France

Puff Pastry

Hundreds of paper-thin layers, built by folding butter into dough again and again.
Fat method
Butter laminated through repeated folds — no yeast involved.
Texture
Crisp, light, and almost entirely hollow from trapped steam.
Examples
Vol-au-vent, palmiers, mille-feuille.
What sets it apart
No raising agent at all — every bit of lift comes from steam alone.
England

Rough Puff Pastry

Puff pastry's faster cousin, with the butter left in rough chunks instead of one smooth block.
Fat method
Butter cut in and folded only a few times, less precisely than classic puff.
Texture
Flaky, but slightly denser and less even than true puff pastry.
Examples
Sausage rolls, jalousies, quick tarts.
What sets it apart
A home-kitchen shortcut that trades some precision for speed.
France & Austria

Croissant Dough

Laminated like puff pastry, but enriched with yeast, milk, and egg.
Fat method
Butter laminated into a yeasted, enriched dough.
Texture
Airy and layered, with a slight chew from the yeast.
Examples
Croissants, pain au chocolat, kouign-amann.
What sets it apart
The only laminated dough that rises from both fermentation and steam.
Denmark, via Austria

Danish Dough

A close relative of croissant dough, enriched further with egg yolk.
Fat method
Butter-laminated and yeasted, with a richer egg content than croissant dough.
Texture
Slightly denser and more cake-like.
Examples
Danish pastries, spandauer.
What sets it apart
Tightened layers that can hold a filling without collapsing.
France & England

Shortcrust (Pâte Brisée)

A crumbly, low-gluten dough made by rubbing fat into flour before any liquid is added.
Fat method
Butter, or a butter-lard mix, rubbed to a sandy crumb — no lamination.
Texture
Short, crumbly, and tender rather than chewy.
Examples
Quiche, savoury tarts, pot-pie lids.
What sets it apart
Mixing is kept to a minimum — "shortening" the gluten is the entire point.
France

Sweet Shortcrust (Pâte Sucrée)

Shortcrust's sweeter sibling, with sugar and egg yolk worked into a cookie-like dough.
Fat method
Butter creamed with sugar first, then flour and egg yolk added.
Texture
Firmer and more biscuit-like than plain shortcrust.
Examples
Fruit tart shells, linzer tarts.
What sets it apart
Can be pressed into a tin rather than rolled out.
India

Mathri / Namkeen Pastry

A short, flaky dough enriched with ghee, used for savoury fried or baked snacks.
Fat method
Ghee rubbed into flour ("moyan"), sometimes with a few rough folds.
Texture
Short and flaky, but sturdy enough to fry.
Examples
Mathri, samosa pastry, kachori dough.
What sets it apart
Built for frying rather than baking, so it's firmer than European shortcrust.
France

Pâte à Choux

A cooked dough, unique for having no lamination or rubbing-in at all.
Fat method
Butter melted into water, flour stirred in over heat, then eggs beaten in off the heat.
Texture
Light and hollow, almost custardy inside once baked.
Examples
Éclairs, profiteroles, gougères, churros.
What sets it apart
Cooked twice — once on the stove, once in the oven — and rises on egg and steam alone.
Greece, Turkey & beyond

Filo / Phyllo

Dough stretched or rolled until it's tissue-paper thin, then layered with fat between the sheets.
Fat method
Brushed between stacked raw sheets — butter or oil — rather than folded in.
Texture
Very crisp, with layers that shatter.
Examples
Baklava, spanakopita, börek.
What sets it apart
Layers are stacked, not folded — each sheet is rolled thin on its own first.
Austria & Germany

Strudel Dough

A stretched dough, pulled by hand until it's almost see-through.
Fat method
Little to none in the dough itself; brushed with melted butter as it's rolled with its filling.
Texture
Thin and crisp outside, with a soft filling inside.
Examples
Apfelstrudel.
What sets it apart
Stretched, not rolled — it needs a strong gluten network to get this thin without tearing.
England

Hot-Water Crust

A sturdy dough made by mixing flour with fat melted into boiling water.
Fat method
Lard (or butter) melted into hot water, then mixed into flour while still hot.
Texture
Firm and mouldable while warm, hardening as it cools.
Examples
Pork pies, game pies.
What sets it apart
Shaped by hand around a mould while still warm, rather than rolled cold.
England

Suet Pastry

A soft dough made with shredded beef or vegetable suet instead of a fat rubbed in cold.
Fat method
Suet stays in small solid pieces through steaming, rather than melting like butter.
Texture
Soft, spongy, and absorbent.
Examples
Steamed puddings, dumplings.
What sets it apart
Usually steamed or boiled rather than baked, so the fat never fully melts in.
Spain & Latin America

Empanada Dough

A firm, rollable dough, sometimes with a splash of wine or vinegar for extra crispness.
Fat method
Butter or lard worked into flour along with egg.
Texture
Sturdy enough to hold a wet filling; crisp when fried or baked.
Examples
Empanadas.
What sets it apart
Built to seal completely around a filling and hold its shape either way it's cooked.
One more thing

Glossary

The terms that came up throughout this guide, gathered in one place.

All-Purpose Flour
Flour milled to a moderate protein content, used as the general default across most shortcrust and everyday baking.
Atta
Whole wheat flour common across South Asia, milled finer than Western whole wheat and including the bran and germ.
Baking Powder
A chemical raising agent that already contains its own acid, reacting with moisture and heat to release carbon dioxide.
Baking Soda
A chemical raising agent that needs an acidic ingredient elsewhere in the dough to react and release carbon dioxide.
Beurrage
The block of butter folded into dough during lamination — the French term for the "butter layer."
Bran
The tough outer coat of a wheat kernel, rich in fibre and left in for whole-wheat and atta flours.
Caramelisation
The browning that happens when sugar alone breaks down under heat, without any protein involved.
Choux
A cooked pastry dough that relies on steam and egg for its rise, rather than any yeast or chemical agent.
Creaming
Beating fat and sugar together until light and aerated — the starting step for many enriched doughs.
Detrempe
The flour-and-water dough that butter is laminated into — the French term for the "dough layer."
Docking
Pricking small holes into rolled-out pastry before baking, so steam can escape and the pastry doesn't puff unevenly.
Endosperm
The starchy bulk of a wheat kernel, containing the proteins that become gluten.
Germ
The nutrient-rich embryo of a wheat kernel, usually removed from refined flour.
Gluten
The elastic protein network formed when a wheat flour's proteins are hydrated and worked. How much forms decides chew versus tenderness.
Hygroscopic
Able to draw in and hold onto moisture from the air — a property of sugar that helps baked pastry stay soft for longer.
Lamination
The process of folding a fat repeatedly through a dough to build up distinct, separate layers.
Maida
A finely milled, refined wheat flour common across South Asia, similar to Western all-purpose or cake flour.
Maillard Reaction
The browning reaction between sugars and proteins at moderate heat, responsible for most of pastry's golden crust colour.
Moyan
Ghee or fat rubbed into flour before liquid is added — the South Asian equivalent of Western "rubbing in."
Rubbing In
Working cold fat into flour with the fingertips until it resembles breadcrumbs, coating the flour to limit gluten formation.
Shortening
Any fat used to "shorten" gluten strands, making a dough tender rather than chewy; also the name for solid vegetable fats sold for this purpose.
Turn
A single fold-and-roll step in laminating a dough. Most laminated pastries go through three to six turns.
Viennoiserie
The category of enriched, laminated, yeasted doughs that sit between bread and pastry, such as croissants and Danish.
Yeast
A living organism that ferments sugars in dough, releasing carbon dioxide slowly as it rises.
