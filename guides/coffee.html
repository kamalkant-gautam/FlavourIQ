THE BASICS

One fruit, four species, an entire world of flavour

Coffee starts life as a cherry on an evergreen shrub of the genus Coffea. Inside each cherry sit two seeds — the "beans" — that are removed, dried, roasted and ground to make the drink. Only a handful of the roughly 130 known Coffea species are grown commercially, and just two, Arabica and Robusta, account for almost everything the world drinks.

Legend of KaldiA 9th-century Ethiopian goat herder said to have discovered coffee after his goats grew energetic from eating cherries
The Bean BeltCommercial coffee grows only in the tropics, roughly between the Tropic of Cancer and Tropic of Capricorn
2 dominant speciesArabica (~60–70% of world production) and Robusta (~30–40%) make up almost the entire market
One of the world's most widely traded agricultural commodities, grown across more than 70 countries
INSIDE THE CHERRY

Anatomy of a coffee bean

What you grind and brew is actually the seed at the centre of a small stone fruit — every layer removed along the way has a name and a job.

Whole cherry
Green bean
Roasted bean
  1. 1
    Outer Skin (Exocarp)The cherry's thin outer peel — green when unripe, turning red or yellow as it matures.
  2. 2
    Pulp (Mesocarp)The sweet, fleshy fruit layer beneath the skin, removed early in processing.
  3. 3
    MucilageA sticky, sugar-rich layer that clings to the parchment — key to fermentation and honey-processed coffees.
  4. 4
    Parchment (Endocarp)A papery protective hull, nicknamed "pergamino," removed by milling after drying.
  5. 5
    Silver Skin (Spermoderm)A thin membrane hugging the bean itself — the papery "chaff" that flakes off during roasting.
  6. 6
    Bean (Endosperm)The seed itself. Two flat-sided beans usually sit face to face inside one cherry.
Peaberry: occasionally only one seed develops inside the cherry, growing round instead of flat-sided. Some roasters sort these out and sell them as a specialty grade.
FROM CHERRY TO GREEN BEAN

Harvesting, processing, sorting & grading

Seven stages turn a ripe cherry into the roasted, ground coffee in your cup — including the processing choice that shapes flavour more than almost anything else.

1. Harvest

Ripe red cherries hand-picked or strip-picked from the branch.

2. Process

Washed, natural, or honey method — separates fruit from seed.

3. Dry

Sun-dried on raised beds or patios to ~11% moisture.

4. Mill / Hull

Parchment and silver skin removed, revealing green coffee.

5. Sort & Grade

By size, density and defects — then scored by cupping.

6. Roast

Heat transforms green beans — light to dark, aroma and colour develop.

7. Grind & Brew

Grind size and method decided by how you're brewing.

HEAT CHANGES EVERYTHING

Roast levels

The same green bean can become three very different coffees, purely from how long and how hot it's roasted.

Same bean, different roast — colour deepens as sugars caramelise and oils migrate outward.

Light Roast

~180–205°C internal temp

Color: Light brown, dry surface, no oil sheen

Tap for details →

Flavour

Bright, fruity, floral, tea-like — origin character comes through strongly, with higher perceived acidity.

Common names

Cinnamon roast, City roast

Best for

Pour-over, Chemex, AeroPress — methods that highlight clarity and nuance

Know this: favoured by the "third wave" specialty scene, especially in Nordic-style filter coffee.

Medium Roast

~210–220°C internal temp

Color: Medium brown, occasional light sheen

Tap for details →

Flavour

Balanced acidity and body, notes of caramel and nuts — the classic "all-purpose" roast.

Common names

City roast, Breakfast blend, American roast

Best for

Drip machines, French press, everyday home brewing

Know this: the most common roast level sold in supermarkets worldwide.

Dark Roast

~225–240°C+ internal temp

Color: Dark brown to near-black, visible oil sheen

Tap for details →

Flavour

Bold, smoky, bittersweet, heavy body — much of the origin's own character is masked by the roast itself.

Common names

Full City, French roast, Italian/Espresso roast

Best for

Espresso, Moka pot, Turkish coffee

Know this: darker roasts actually have slightly less caffeine by volume than lighter roasts of the same weight, since beans lose mass and expand as they roast.
NOT ALL COFFEE IS THE SAME PLANT

The four commercial coffee species

Almost everything you drink comes from just two of these — the other two are niche, but worth knowing.

Arabica

Coffea arabica

Origin: Ethiopian highlands

Tap for details →

Growing conditions

High altitude (600–2000m), cooler temperatures — more delicate, more susceptible to pests, frost and disease.

Caffeine & flavour

Lower caffeine (~1.2–1.5%); smoother, more complex, aromatic, higher acidity

Share of world production

~60–70%

Know this: major producers include Brazil, Colombia, Ethiopia and much of Central America.

Robusta

Coffea canephora

Origin: Congo basin, Central/West Sub-Saharan Africa

Tap for details →

Growing conditions

Lower altitude (200–800m), hotter climates — hardier, more disease-resistant, higher yields.

Caffeine & flavour

Higher caffeine (~2.2–2.7%); bolder, more bitter, earthy/woody, produces thicker crema

Share of world production

~30–40%

Know this: the backbone of espresso blends (for crema) and most instant coffee, thanks to cost and higher solubility. Major producers: Vietnam, Brazil, Uganda.

Liberica

Coffea liberica

Origin: West Africa (Liberia); grown in the Philippines & Malaysia

Tap for details →

Growing conditions

Tolerates heat and humidity better than Arabica; large, irregularly shaped beans.

Caffeine & flavour

Distinct floral, fruity, sometimes smoky or woody aroma unlike either Arabica or Robusta.

Share of world production

Under 2%

Know this: known locally as "Barako" in the Philippines, where it has a small but devoted following.

Excelsa

Coffea excelsa (a Liberica variant)

Origin: Southeast Asia

Tap for details →

Growing conditions

Hardy, drought-tolerant tree; now classified by many botanists as a variety of Liberica rather than its own species.

Caffeine & flavour

Tart and fruity with a complex profile that blends light-roast brightness with dark-roast depth.

Share of world production

Very small — mostly used to add complexity to blends

Know this: grown commercially in Vietnam, the Philippines and parts of India.
TYPES OF PREPARATION

Brewing methods, from espresso to instant

Same beans, wildly different cups — grind size, water temperature, pressure and time are the four levers every method plays with differently.

Tap a card to open it. Filter by how it's brewed.

Espresso

Italy, early 1900s

Invented by: Luigi Bezzera (1901), refined by Achille Gaggia (1940s)

Tap for method & flavour →

Method

Fine grind · 18–20g in, 36–40g out (1:2) · 9 bar pressure · 90–96°C · 25–30 sec

Flavour

Concentrated, syrupy, with a golden-brown crema layer on top.

Fun fact: crema — the foam on top — only forms under real pressure, which is what separates true espresso from strong drip coffee.

French Press

France / Italy, patented 1929

Also called: Press pot, cafetière

Tap for method & flavour →

Method

Coarse grind · 1:15 ratio · steep 4 min · plunge slowly · full immersion

Flavour

Full-bodied and rich — the metal mesh lets natural oils through, unlike paper filters.

Fun fact: because it's unfiltered, French press coffee carries more of the oily compounds that paper filters usually trap.

Pour-Over (V60)

Paper filter: Germany 1908 · Cone: Japan, 1980s

Also called: Hario V60, Kalita Wave

Tap for method & flavour →

Method

Medium-fine grind · 1:16 ratio · 30 sec bloom, then slow spiral pours · 2.5–3.5 min total · 92–96°C

Flavour

Clean, bright, highlights subtle origin notes.

Fun fact: Melitta Bentz, a German housewife, invented the paper coffee filter in 1908 using blotting paper from her son's schoolbook.

Chemex

USA, invented 1941

Invented by: Peter Schlumbohm, a German chemist

Tap for method & flavour →

Method

Medium-coarse grind · 1:15–1:17 ratio · thick proprietary paper filter · 4–5 min total

Flavour

Exceptionally clean and light-bodied — the thick filter removes almost all oils and sediment.

Fun fact: the Chemex's hourglass design is part of the Museum of Modern Art's permanent collection.

AeroPress

USA, invented 2005

Invented by: Alan Adler (also invented the Aerobie flying disc)

Tap for method & flavour →

Method

Medium-fine grind · steep 1–2 min · press ~30 sec · 80–85°C · many recipe variations

Flavour

Clean like a pour-over but with fuller body; extremely versatile.

Fun fact: it's popular enough to have its own annual World AeroPress Championship.

Moka Pot

Italy, invented 1933

Invented by: Luigi De Ponti, for Alfonso Bialetti

Tap for method & flavour →

Method

Fine-medium grind · steam pressure pushes water up through the grounds into the top chamber

Flavour

Strong and bold — semi-espresso character, though with less crema and pressure than a true machine.

Fun fact: its iconic octagonal aluminium shape hasn't changed since the 1930s and sits in nearly every Italian kitchen.

Cold Brew

Roots in Kyoto-style drip; popularised in the US, 2000s–2010s

Style: Steeped cold, no heat at all

Tap for method & flavour →

Method

Coarse grind · steeped in room-temp or cold water 12–24 hrs · filtered · served over ice or diluted

Flavour

Smooth, naturally sweeter, low acidity, less bitter than hot-brewed coffee.

Fun fact: cold brew concentrate keeps well refrigerated for up to two weeks.

Siphon / Vacuum Pot

Germany/France, 1830s–40s; refined in Japan

Also called: Vacuum pot, syphon

Tap for method & flavour →

Method

Vapor pressure pushes water up to meet the grounds; removing the heat pulls the brew back down through a filter

Flavour

Clean, aromatic, tea-like clarity with a fuller body than pour-over.

Fun fact: as much theatre as brewing — often performed tableside in Japanese kissaten cafés.

Turkish Coffee

Ottoman Empire, 16th century

Vessel: Ibrik / cezve — small long-handled copper pot

Tap for method & flavour →

Method

Extremely fine, powder-like grind · simmered slowly with water (often sugar) · brought to foam multiple times · served unfiltered

Flavour

Intense and thick, often spiced with cardamom; grounds settle at the bottom of the cup.

Fun fact: recognised by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage tradition in Turkey.

Drip Machine

USA, popularised from the 1970s

Also called: Auto-drip, filter coffee maker

Tap for method & flavour →

Method

Medium grind · 1:16–1:18 ratio · machine drips hot water evenly over grounds into a carafe

Flavour

Consistent and mild — built for convenience over nuance.

Fun fact: the most common way coffee is brewed at home across the US and much of the Western world.

Instant Coffee

Pre-brewed, then dehydrated

Made via: Spray-drying or freeze-drying brewed coffee into powder or granules

Method

Just add hot water — no brewing equipment needed

Flavour

Milder and flatter than fresh-brewed, but fast, consistent, and shelf-stable for years.

Fun fact: usually made from Robusta beans, chosen for cost efficiency and how readily they dissolve.

RITUAL & SERVICE

Coffee around the world

The same bean, shaped into six very different rituals — each with its own etiquette worth knowing.

Italy — Espresso Bar

Quick espresso taken standing "al banco" at the bar; cappuccino is traditionally an morning-only order, never after a meal.

Ethiopia — Coffee Ceremony

Green beans roasted fresh in a pan before guests, ground by hand, and brewed in a clay jebena — served across three ceremonial rounds.

Turkey — Turkish Coffee

Brewed in an ibrik, served with a glass of water and Turkish delight — the leftover grounds are traditionally read for fortune-telling.

Vietnam — Cà Phê Sữa Đá

Strong robusta drips slowly through a small metal phin filter over sweetened condensed milk, then poured over ice.

Japan — Precision Brewing

Pour-over and siphon methods refined to near-ritual precision, alongside a ubiquitous culture of canned and bottled coffee vending machines.

Brazil — Cafezinho

The world's largest coffee producer for over 150 years; a small, sweet, strong "cafezinho" is a routine gesture of hospitality.

South India — Filter Coffee

Introduced via Chikmagalur in the 1600s, when legend says the pilgrim Baba Budan smuggled seven coffee seeds out of Yemen. A metal filter drips a strong, dark-roast decoction — often blended with chicory — which is then mixed with hot milk and sugar, and poured back and forth between tumbler and davara from a height to cool and froth it. The result is strong, sweet, and slightly earthy, best known today as the daily ritual behind South India's "meter coffee."

TERROIR MATTERS

Geographical impact on coffee

Altitude, latitude and soil shape flavour as much as processing does — the same idea winemakers call terroir.

THE BEAN BELT
~25°N to 30°S
Americas

Brazil, Colombia, Central America

Moderate altitude, volcanic soils in parts — balanced, nutty, chocolatey cups with medium acidity. Brazil alone grows roughly a third of the world's coffee.

Africa & Arabia

Ethiopia, Kenya, Yemen

Coffee's birthplace region — high altitude and heirloom varieties give bright, floral, fruity, wine-like acidity prized by specialty roasters.

Asia-Pacific

Indonesia, Vietnam, Papua New Guinea

Humid climates and wet-hulling processing common in the region produce earthy, full-bodied, low-acidity coffees with spicy, herbal notes.

WHAT'S ACTUALLY IN THE CUP

Coffee's key compounds

Most of coffee's effects trace back to a handful of naturally occurring compounds, in amounts that shift with roast level and brewing method.

Caffeine

Coffee's signature stimulant. Brewing method affects it as much as bean type — longer contact time and no filter both tend to raise it.

Chlorogenic acids

Plant antioxidants found in green coffee, partly broken down by roasting — lighter roasts generally retain more.

Trigonelline

Contributes to coffee's aroma and breaks down during roasting into niacin (vitamin B3).

Diterpenes (cafestol & kahweol)

Found in unfiltered coffee like French press and Turkish coffee; paper filters (pour-over, drip) trap most of these compounds.

CAFFEINE, MOSTLY REMOVED

Understanding decaf

Decaffeinated coffee is regular coffee with roughly 97% or more of its caffeine removed — always done to the green bean, before roasting, using one of three main processes.

Tap a process to see how it works.

Swiss Water Process

Chemical-free, water only

Uses: Hot water and a green coffee extract (GCE)

Tap for how it works →

How it works

Green beans soak in hot water, which pulls out both caffeine and flavour compounds. That water — now a flavour-saturated "green coffee extract" — passes through an activated carbon filter that traps only the caffeine molecules. A fresh batch of beans is then soaked in this already flavour-saturated water: since it's fully saturated with flavour but stripped of caffeine, only caffeine (via osmosis) moves out of the new beans, leaving the flavour compounds largely intact.

Know this: no chemical solvents are involved at any stage, which makes it a favourite for organic-certified decaf.

CO₂ Process

Supercritical carbon dioxide

Uses: Pressurised CO₂ as the solvent

Tap for how it works →

How it works

Moistened green beans sit in a sealed chamber with CO₂ pressurised into a "supercritical" state — dense like a liquid, but flowing like a gas. In that state it selectively bonds to caffeine molecules without pulling out much flavour. The pressure is then released, the CO₂ turns back into gas and simply evaporates off, carrying the caffeine with it, and is recaptured for reuse.

Know this: favoured for large-scale commercial decaf since it preserves flavour well and leaves no chemical residue.

Solvent-Based Process

Direct or indirect method

Uses: Methylene chloride or ethyl acetate

Tap for how it works →

How it works

In the direct method, steamed green beans are rinsed repeatedly with a solvent that bonds to caffeine, then steamed again to remove any trace of it. In the indirect method, beans are soaked in hot water first; that water is treated with the solvent to pull out the caffeine, then returned to the beans to reabsorb the leftover flavour. Ethyl acetate occurs naturally in some fruits, so this version is sometimes marketed as a "natural process."

Know this: residual solvent levels are tightly regulated and tested to stay within food-safety limits well below any risk threshold.

What decaffeination actually changes

Flavour

Swiss Water and CO₂ methods tend to preserve origin character closely; solvent-based methods can shift aroma and flavour slightly more.

Caffeine, not gone entirely

Decaf isn't caffeine-free — it typically still carries about 2–12mg per cup, versus roughly 95mg in a regular cup of drip coffee.

Antioxidants stay mostly intact

Chlorogenic acids and other antioxidants aren't targeted by any decaffeination process, so decaf keeps most of coffee's other health-associated compounds.

Who reaches for it

A common choice for pregnancy, caffeine sensitivity, sleep concerns, or simply an evening cup — without giving up the ritual of coffee itself.

KEEP IT FRESH

Storage & freshness notes

Grind right before brewing

Ground coffee oxidises within minutes; whole beans hold their flavour far longer than pre-ground coffee.

Airtight, dark, cool

Store beans away from heat, light and moisture — an opaque, sealed container beats a clear jar on the counter.

Skip the fridge

Condensation from temperature swings does more harm than good — save the freezer only for long-term, tightly sealed portions.

Use within 2–4 weeks of roasting

Look for a "roasted on" date, not just "best by" — freshly roasted beans also need a few days to rest and release CO₂ before brewing.

SPEAK THE LANGUAGE

Glossary of coffee terms

Tap a term to reveal its definition.

Crema
The golden-brown foam on top of espresso, formed from emulsified oils and CO₂ under pressure.
Extraction
The process of dissolving flavour compounds from grounds into water. Under-extracted tastes sour and weak; over-extracted tastes bitter and harsh.
TDS
Total Dissolved Solids — a measure of coffee concentration, used by baristas to dial in a brew's strength precisely.
Single-origin
Coffee from one country, region or farm, kept unblended so its specific character comes through.
Blend
A mix of beans from different origins or roast levels, combined for a consistent, balanced flavour.
Third Wave
The modern specialty coffee movement that treats coffee like wine — focused on origin, processing and precise brewing.
Cupping
A standardised tasting and scoring method professionals use to evaluate coffee quality.
Green Coffee
Unroasted coffee beans, still pale green, before any roasting has taken place.
Bloom
The initial release of CO₂ and swelling of grounds the moment hot water first touches fresh coffee.
Decaf
Coffee with roughly 97%+ of its caffeine removed, typically via the Swiss Water, CO₂, or solvent-based process.
